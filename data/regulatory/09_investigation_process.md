# Alert to disposition: the investigation process

## Alert generation
Transaction monitoring produces alerts from rules, statistical models, or both.
A large majority of alerts are not indicative of suspicious activity. Published
analysis has placed the false positive rate of transaction monitoring systems
in the region of ninety to ninety-five percent. Triage exists to allocate
investigative effort proportionately, not to suppress alerts.

## Triage
The first review establishes whether the alerted activity is explained by the
customer's known profile and history. A triage decision must record the reason,
whether the outcome is escalation or closure. Closing an alert without a
recorded rationale is a documentation failure that will be identified in
examination regardless of whether the closure was correct.

## Investigation
An escalated alert becomes a case. The investigator assembles the transaction
history over an appropriate lookback period, the customer due diligence file,
counterparty information, results of sanctions and politically exposed person
screening, adverse media where relevant, and the history of prior alerts and
filings on the same subject.

## Disposition
The case concludes in one of three ways: closure with a documented rationale;
filing a suspicious activity report; or continued monitoring with a defined
review date. Where a report is filed and the activity continues, a continuing
report is generally expected at defined intervals.

## Quality assurance
A sample of closed alerts should be independently reviewed. Sampling rates are
risk-based, with higher-value or higher-risk queues sampled more heavily. Where
any part of the process is automated, the sampling regime must cover the
automated decisions specifically.
